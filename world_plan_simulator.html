    How the simulation works

    1. People. Ten regions with populations from the UN's World Population Prospects 2024 (mid-2025, ≈8.2 billion in total).
    2. Pages. Each person writes one page a day. What a region writes about comes from a baseline weight per need, plus seasons (heat, winter energy, South Asia's monsoon, the school year), fourteen random crises, and daily noise.
    3. Needs. Pages add up into person-days per need. Each region's own priorities are sharpened (squared and renormalised) so top concerns stand out.
    4. Plan. One budget split across eight packages, weighted either by people or equally by region. "Shared core + local top-ups" spends 60% on the shared plan and lets each region spend 40% on its own top needs.
    5. Vote. Each region's support depends on how closely the plan matches its needs (overlap), passed through a normal curve to allow for disagreement inside every region.

    What this can and cannot tell you. No real journals are used. The baseline weights, crisis timing and support curve are assumptions chosen to be plausible, not measured. The local top-up option wins partly by construction, since it gives every region more of what it asked for. The real question it raises is whether people would accept 40% of a world budget being spent regionally. Even here no option reaches 100% in every region: unanimity is not what a vote of 8 billion produces.
